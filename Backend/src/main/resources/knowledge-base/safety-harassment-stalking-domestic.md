# Harassment, Stalking & Domestic Violence — Guidance

General information for recognizing and responding to harassment,
stalking, and domestic violence. This is not legal advice — for guidance
specific to your situation, contact the National Commission for Women
helpline (14490 or 7827170170), the Women Helpline (181), or a lawyer.

## Recognizing stalking and harassment

Stalking isn't always obvious at first — it can include repeated
unwanted contact (calls, messages, showing up uninvited), being followed
or watched, unwanted gifts, or someone monitoring your social media or
location closely. It often escalates gradually. Trust the pattern, not
just any single incident: if someone's behavior is making you feel
watched, unsafe, or unable to say no, that's worth taking seriously and
documenting, regardless of whether any one incident alone seems "bad
enough" to report.

## Documenting incidents

Keep a private record: dates, times, what happened, and any messages,
calls, or photos as evidence — screenshots with visible timestamps are
especially useful. Don't delete threatening or harassing messages even if
they're upsetting to see; they may be needed later. If safe to do so, tell
someone you trust what's happening so there's a witness to the pattern,
not just your own account of it.

## If you're being followed right now

Don't go home directly, since that confirms where you live. Head to a
public, populated place — a shop, a police station, anywhere with people
and ideally cameras. Call someone and stay on the phone, or trigger
UnSocial's SOS Alert so your location is shared with your contacts in
real time. If you can safely note the person's description, clothing, or
vehicle, do so — but your safety comes first, not gathering evidence.

## Domestic violence

Domestic violence includes physical, emotional, sexual, or financial
abuse from a partner or family member, and it's rarely a single isolated
incident. If you're in this situation, you're not required to have "left
already" or have a complete plan to reach out for help — the 181 Women
Helpline and NCW helpline are available specifically to talk through
safety planning, not just to take reports after the fact. If you're
building a safety plan, having key documents (ID, money, a packed bag)
accessible somewhere safe outside the home, and a trusted person who
knows your situation, are commonly recommended starting points — a
counselor on these helplines can help tailor a plan to your specific
circumstances.

## Reporting

You can file a police report (FIR) at any police station regardless of
where the incident happened — this is called a Zero FIR, and the
station is required to forward it to the correct jurisdiction. You're
entitled to a free copy of any FIR filed. The NCW and state women's
commissions can also help escalate complaints if you face difficulty at a
local station. None of this requires you to decide everything at once —
helplines like 181 and the NCW's 14490 exist to help you figure out next
steps, not just to process a decision you've already made.

## Supporting someone else

If someone confides in you about harassment, stalking, or abuse, believe
them, avoid pressuring them into a specific course of action on your
timeline, and ask what kind of help they actually want rather than
assuming. Offer to go with them to report something or to call a helpline
together if they'd like company. Avoid contacting the person they've
described as a threat yourself, as this can escalate risk to the person
who confided in you.
